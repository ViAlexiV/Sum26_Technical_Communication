###Final Essay

Before this course, I perceived documentation as a simple transfer of facts: the main goal was to describe the steps clearly and sequentially. However, in practice, it turned out to be much more complex. The iterative process of "Write → Review → Evaluate → Repeat" demonstrated that a good text relies not only on the right words but also on structure, formatting, and user care. Below, I want to summarize my learning experience and how my approach to writing and reviewing texts has evolved.  

### Step 1. Creating My Own Texts and Basic Structure
When I wrote my first assignments - guides on digitizing analog video and developing color negative film I approached the task with an engineer's mindset. I provided clear lists of equipment, detailed the procedures, and added visual Warnings to protect the reader from critical errors, such as exposing the film to light. I believed the logic was structured perfectly. But it wasn't until I started evaluating other people's work (peer review) that I realized how technical text is perceived from the outside.  

### Step 2. From Spotting Typos to Structural Analysis
While reviewing the spaghetti cooking guide, I relied heavily on the materials learnt during lectures explaining the difference between structural review and simple proofreading. I stopped hunting for typos and analyzed the text systematically:  

* Document Logic: I noticed that the author promised a book review in the introduction but forgot to include it in the main body.  

* Tone and Style: I pointed out unnecessary subjective evaluations (e.g., "essential skill," "easy-to-follow"). Technical text must remain objective and be based on facts rather than emotions.  

* Usability: I suggested highlighting a critical action (reserving a cup of pasta water) in a separate visual callout block. In real-world scenarios, people often drain all the water before reading the sub-steps to the end. This realization was my first practical step toward User-Focused Documentation—understanding that the text should actively prevent user errors.  

### Step 3. Implementing Docs-as-Code and Infostyle
My approach fully solidified during the review of the document discussing body weight changes. Here, I applied the entire toolkit acquired during the course:  

* Format (Docs-as-Code): The author submitted the document as a PDF. I was displeased surprised to see the pdf file. Documentation should live close to the codebase, be easily editable, and support version control. Therefore, it recommended to convert the text to the Markdown format.  

* User Focus: The text resembled a blog article. I advised transforming it into a How-to guide or a Frequently Asked Questions format. Users open documentation to solve a specific problem, not just for the sake of reading.  

* Infostyle: I suggested removing abstract reasoning, replacing lists of nouns with strong action verbs (Actions over Nouns), and adding specifics (e.g., providing an exact sleep duration instead of the vague phrase "adequate sleep").  

Reflecting on the peer review process, I must admit that I was somewhat disappointed by the lack of professionalism and technical depth in the texts I was assigned to evaluate. I expected to review highly structured, technical documents, but instead, I often encountered casual recipes or blog-like essays.

On a much more positive note, I discovered a genuine passion for writing documentation in the Docs-as-Code format. Treating text like code—using Markdown and integrating it seamlessly into a developer-like workflow—was an incredibly engaging and rewarding experience. It made the documentation process feel logical, modern, and highly efficient.

### Conclusion
The course provided me with an excellent set of practical tools. I learned not only how to write straightforward instructions but also how to apply Docs-as-Code principles, Infostyle, and a UX-oriented approach to technical writing.
